Qi et al. compared music-genre classification using traditional GTZAN low-level audio features versus higher-level Spotify features to understand the trade-off between accuracy and interpretability. [1] They reported the classic MFCC baseline of 61 % accuracy on GTZAN as a historical reference point for genre recognition. [1] More advanced models cited in their study include a convolutional neural network that achieved 81.8 % accuracy on GTZAN and a stacked ensemble combining SVM, RVM and Decision Tree that reached 87 % accuracy on the same dataset. [1] The authors concluded that while Spotify’s perceptual features are more human-interpretable, GTZAN’s timbral descriptors still deliver higher predictive performance in current machine-learning pipelines. [1]

Karunakaran and Arya proposed a scalable hybrid classifier built on Apache Spark that combines several machine-learning algorithms with Essentia-based audio feature extraction. [2] Using the GTZAN dataset, their hybrid ensemble, which integrates models such as KNN, SVM, QDA, Naïve Bayes and neural networks after PCA dimensionality reduction, achieved about 90 % accuracy, outperforming each individual classifier. [2] When the same architecture was evaluated on a much larger Free Music Archive subset of roughly 20 000 tracks across multiple genres, the accuracy was around 70 %, reflecting the increased difficulty of large-scale, fine-grained genre discrimination. [2] The authors emphasized that Spark-based parallelization allowed this level of accuracy to be obtained with practical training times, demonstrating that distributed processing can support real-world music-analysis workloads. [2]

Humayra et al. investigated automatic genre classification for Bengali songs using a set of traditional machine-learning and deep-learning models. [3] Their experimental results show that Random Forest reached 71.22 % accuracy, while SVM-RBF and SVM-Linear obtained 74.35 % and 71.52 %, respectively, on the Bengali music dataset. [3] Additional classifiers such as SGD (64.78 %), Decision Tree (61.26 %) and K-Nearest Neighbours (65.57 %) produced lower but still competitive performance. [3] Among neural approaches, a multilayer perceptron (MLP) achieved 75.61 % accuracy, and the best overall model was a Deep Neural Network with 83.65 % accuracy, indicating that deeper architectures are better at capturing the melodic and rhythmic structures specific to Bengali music. [3]

Silla Jr., Koerich and Kaestner proposed a space–time decomposition framework for music-genre classification on the Latin Music Database, evaluating multiple classifiers across several segmentation strategies. [4] For global space–time decompositions, they reported overall average accuracies (OAA) such as 49.63 % for J48, 59.96 % for 3-NN, 61.03 % for MLP and 30.79 % for SVM, with recall-rate (RR) values reaching 65.06 % for SVM and 61.12 % for 3-NN. [4] When analyzing individual segments, middle-segment experiments yielded FSRR values like 62.55 % for 3-NN and 63.50 % RR for SVM, while beginning and end segments generally showed lower accuracies. [4] The authors concluded that space–time ensembles exploiting beginning, middle and end fragments of songs systematically outperform models trained on the entire piece, demonstrating that temporal decomposition is beneficial for Latin-genre recognition. [4]

Pathania and Kaur evaluated both spectrogram-based deep models and feature-engineering-based classical models for music-genre classification. [5] Among spectrogram approaches, VGG-16 used for transfer learning achieved 0.63 accuracy, VGG-16 with fine-tuning reached 0.64, and a feed-forward neural-network baseline obtained 0.43, with corresponding F-scores of 0.61, 0.61 and 0.33. [5] For feature-engineering pipelines, Logistic Regression, Random Forest, SVM and Extreme Gradient Boosting achieved accuracies of 0.53, 0.54, 0.57 and 0.59, respectively, with XGBoost providing the best F-score (0.55) and AUC (0.865) among these models. [5] Their strongest overall model was an ensemble combining VGG-16 with XGBoost, which achieved 0.65 accuracy, 0.62 F-score and an AUC of 0.894, highlighting the benefits of fusing deep spectrogram features with gradient-boosted decision trees. [5]

Devapatla Bhargavi, Magoo and Kaur focused on classifying music genres and recommending songs using standard supervised-learning algorithms. [6] In their experiments, the Random Forest classifier obtained an overall accuracy of 0.81, providing balanced precision and recall across genres. [6] The K-Nearest Neighbours model performed even better with 0.89 accuracy, though KNN can be more computationally intensive at inference time due to its instance-based nature. [6] The Support Vector Machine classifier achieved 0.75 accuracy, trailing the tree-based and instance-based methods, while XGBoost delivered the best performance with 0.92 accuracy, confirming the effectiveness of gradient-boosted ensembles for tabular audio-feature data. [6]

Salian K. et al. compared four classifiers—CNN, FNN, KNN and SVM—for music-genre classification and reported both training and testing accuracies. [7] The convolutional neural network achieved a training accuracy of 0.94 and a test accuracy of 0.81, indicating good generalization compared with the other models. [7] The feed-forward neural network (FNN) reached 0.95 on the training set but only 0.53 on the test set, suggesting substantial overfitting. [7] Similarly, K-Nearest Neighbours obtained 0.99 training accuracy but only 0.53 test accuracy, while SVM achieved 0.96 training and 0.59 testing accuracy, both reflecting weaker generalization than the CNN. [7] The authors concluded that CNNs are more robust for this task, as they retain high test accuracy while avoiding the severe overfitting seen in FNN and KNN. [7]

Elbir et al. investigated music-genre classification and recommendation using a combination of CNN-based spectrogram models and temporal sequence models. [8] Their CNN configurations achieved accuracies in the range of 83 % to 91 %, depending on architectural choices and data-augmentation strategies. [8] In addition, they employed Hidden Markov Models for temporal acoustic modeling and reported genre-classification accuracies exceeding 90 % on the Magnatunes dataset. [8] The study showed that while CNNs provide strong performance on static time–frequency representations, HMM-based temporal modeling can further improve recognition of genre-specific progression patterns, which in turn enhances the quality of downstream recommendation. [8]

Yuwono, Tjiandra and Manuaba analyzed music-genre classification using Support Vector Machines trained on features derived from Empirical Mode Decomposition and intrinsic mode functions. [9] Working with a relatively small Kaggle dataset containing five genres, they evaluated several SVM kernel variants and found that the linear SVM performed best. [9] The highest accuracy they reported was 46.9 %, which is substantially lower than the accuracies achieved by deep-learning and ensemble models in other studies. [9] The authors attributed this modest performance to the limited dataset size and the low-dimensional feature representation, arguing that richer spectral features and larger corpora are needed for SVMs to be competitive in music-genre tasks. [9]

Patil et al. compared a range of classical and deep models for music-genre classification using MFCC- and spectrum-based features. [10] On the GTZAN dataset, they reported that SVM achieved 80 % accuracy, KNN reached 77.18 %, and Naïve Bayes obtained 76.08 %, indicating that SVM is the strongest of the traditional classifiers they evaluated. [10] A CNN-based ensemble further improved performance to 84 % accuracy, highlighting the advantage of learning directly from time–frequency representations. [10] They also cited a dynamic-frame SVM approach evaluated on a separate custom six-genre dataset, where the accuracy reached 98 %, although this result is not directly comparable to the 10-genre GTZAN experiments. [10] Overall, their results reinforce the pattern that deep or ensemble models typically outperform single classical classifiers when sufficient data and informative spectral features are available. [10]
