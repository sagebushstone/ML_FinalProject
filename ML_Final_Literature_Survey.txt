Research in music genre classification has investigated a diverse array of datasets, machine learning algorithms, and feature extraction methods, leading to a wide variety of accuracy results. One of the most frequently utilized datasets is GTZAN, comprising 1,000 audio recordings spanning ten evenly distributed genres. Its popularity derives from its ease of use and uniform structure. Additional datasets, including the Free Music Archive (FMA), Magnatunes, regional-language music corpora, and multiple Spotify-derived Kaggle datasets, have also been extensively utilized. These datasets vary considerably in terms of size, balance, and audio quality, which directly impact the efficacy of the model. Smaller or imbalanced datasets, particularly those based on Spotify’s metadata rather than raw audio, generally result in significantly reduced accuracy.

A diverse range of machine learning algorithms has been employed in the literature. Traditional models such as K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Decision Trees, Naïve Bayes, and Random Forests typically demonstrate moderate predictive performance, with accuracy rates generally ranging from 33% to 55%. These lower values generally occur when models depend exclusively on features derived from Spotify metadata—such as energy, danceability, loudness, speechiness, and valence—which are broad descriptors and do not encompass the detailed timbral or spectral attributes necessary for accurate genre classification.

More sophisticated classical algorithms, such as Support Vector Machines (SVM) and XGBoost, demonstrate enhanced performance when trained on more comprehensive feature sets, including MFCCs, spectral centroid, spectral bandwidth, chroma features, zero-crossing rate, and other frequency-domain characteristics. In such instances, the accuracy typically varies between 60% and 80%, depending on the feature engineering and preprocessing techniques employed. SVM, in particular, demonstrates strong performance when audio features are distinctive, although the results may still fluctuate depending on the complexity and balance of the dataset.

The most accurate results documented in the literature are achieved by deep learning models, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) trained on spectrogram or Mel-spectrogram representations of audio signals. These models inherently acquire hierarchical feature representations and consistently attain accuracy levels ranging from 85% to 94%. Hybrid architectures such as CNN–LSTM or CNN–BiLSTM further enhance the extraction of sequential features and have achieved reported accuracies ranging from 92% to 96% on specific benchmark datasets. Several advanced sparse representation and neural-architectural models also exceed the 90% threshold.

Overall, the literature indicates that classification accuracy is highly influenced by the nature of the features, the extent and diversity of the dataset, and the complexity of the learning model. Metadata-based and small-dataset analyses demonstrate reduced performance, whereas deep learning methods utilizing raw audio data reliably yield superior outcomes. These findings underscore the significance of feature richness and model architecture in attaining effective genre classification.
